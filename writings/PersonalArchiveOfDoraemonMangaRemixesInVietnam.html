Personal Archive of "Doraemon" Manga Remixes in Vietnam

Looks like I'm gonna have to explain the whole thing from start to finish.

Alright... here we go. [inhales]

Doraemon chế (also written Đôrêmon chế) is a Vietnamese digital subculture that peaked in the early 2010s, wherein enthusiasts altered existing "Doraemon" scanlations to produce new works. The process was straightforward: grab digital scans of the original manga, load them into an image editor—most commonly MS Paint (or pirated Photoshop for those who could manage it), scrub away the existing words, and compose new dialogue to transform the panels into unrelated tales. Because Vietnam's got an immense, dedicated "Doraemon" fandom, naturally there is a lot of remix material.

Over time, this style of content eventually became a kind of indigenous web-comic shorthand on the Vietnamese web. The iconic characters became vehicles for commentary on study stress, relationships, mundane routines, trending topics, public figures, governance issues, and fantastical what-if plots. Corny declarations of love and sentimental one-liners? They'd put those words in Nobita's mouth. The nightmare of national high school exams? Fans would weave together disconnected manga panels to construct biting fanfics about systemic unfairness. Whatever social ill needed exposing, or whichever public figure had become the butt of every Vietnamese forum joke, the medium could handle the critique.

The panels' self-contained nature and sparse backgrounds meant the creative math worked out to near-infinity; you could yank panels from completely different stories, Frankenstein them together, and coherence wouldn't suffer. You could theoretically blank out a "Bleach" page instead, but "Doraemon" delivered exponentially more liberty, creative capacity, and room for conceptual play. The remix scene is reminiscent of how meme culture mines "The Simpsons", "SpongeBob", or any major Western property for templates—except applied to "Doraemon" and escalated to the absolute ceiling. Yeah, it's that big. Why limit oneself to cartoon stills when the complete manga was there for the taking? And since "Doraemon" held the status of a quasi-sacred cultural import in Vietnam, everyone ate it up.

To give you an idea of the scene's reach, consider that the entire movement unfolded primarily on Facebook (Vietnam's national social network). As early as 2011, multiple groups and pages were already thriving: one remix group alone had grown beyond 50,000 members, and another contemporary tally from 2011 reported an almost absurd membership count: 97,344 by July. Separate research conducted on May 30, 2011 documented another page at 80,438 fans. Across its opening three months, the page generated 339 posts, but the user base contributed 9,939. Crunch those numbers and the ratio comes out to roughly thirty times more user-generated content than official posts. A period survey further broke down the main page's labour structure, identifying dedicated contributors who sourced panels, edited video, manipulated images, illustrated, and coloured artwork.

Yes, there were people who actively spent their leisure hours scouring scans, extracting individual panels, and sharing them so anyone could create.

By contemporary standards, the "Doraemon" remix movement is finished. The bandwagon participants and the subculture's standout figures—everyone who engaged while in their late teens or early twenties—have all progressed onward. They're still online, but they no longer look back at or claim ownership of their impact on the nation's internet culture. Growing up brought bigger obligations and fuller lives. At the same time, the preferences of online communities have evolved so radically that publishing a remix today—which demands no real technical hurdle; MS Paint endures, and GIMP costs nothing—would yield almost no traction. Everyone would dismiss it as a feeble attempt to revive a meme that has long since died... except you're one of those rare original figures from the subculture's apex whose work has morphed so dramatically that the contemporary output feels disconnected from the original spirit (kinda like SMG4 in that regard). Creating the same early-2010s remix experience isn't genuine content creation so much as crafting a Rosetta Stone replica whose sole purpose is making the ancient text more legible to modern viewers.

Why am I bothering with an archive?

Perhaps unexpectedly, I was one of the people who got swept up in that wave. I created a number of remixes starting from my elementary years, persisting through junior high, and then moving onto Facebook once I came of age for it. Those days linger in my memory.

The creations were low-effort—not in the current internet sense of "mass-producing identical dreck throughout the 2020s", but in the older sense of "launch MS Paint, make some basic edits, and consider the task complete". Crude and quick, yet endlessly entertaining. Nothing prior had matched it. This was my childhood, and I want to preserve it as a testament to a life that was beautiful.

What follows isn't a scientifically precise English rendering of the Vietnamese originals. These are localised translations—as in "DAMN CRUNCHYROLL, THEIR DUB LINES INSERTED WOKE NONSENSE AGAIN!!!" The wording you see won't always mirror the Vietnamese source material exactly. You can gripe about that if you want, but the jokes work better when they don't require fluency in Vietnamese. For especially thorny passages, I may fall back on footnotes.
