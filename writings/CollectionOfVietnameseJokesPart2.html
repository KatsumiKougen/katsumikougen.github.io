Finally, it was the doctor's turn. Terrified, he stammered, "I'm not ripe yet! I'm still completely green, so I can't fall!"

The patients shook their heads. "This one's still green, so we'll just have to throw stones at him until he drops!" And they began pelting him with rocks until the bruised and battered doctor finally let go and fell to the ground.

The Student's Calendar

A year has 365 days, but a typical student's year breaks down a bit differently:

  • Saturdays: There are 52 Saturdays in a year, which everyone knows are meant for resting and "exploring the world". That leaves 313 days.
  • Summer Vacation: The next 50 days. It's too hot to study anyway; beach trips and hanging out are what summer is for. That leaves 263 days.
  • Sleep: 8 hours of sleep every single day adds up to 122 days lost per year, leaving 141 days.
  • Recreation: Spending 1 hour a day on sports and relaxation (great for health) takes 15 days, leaving 126 days.
  • Meals: 2 hours a day for eating and culinary pleasures takes 30 days, leaving 96 days.
  • Socialising: 1 hour a day chatting with friends takes 15 days, leaving 81 days.
  • Exams: At least 35 days spent on tests and evaluations, leaving 46 days.
  • Holidays & Festivals: Roughly 40 days of festivities throughout the year, leaving 6 days.
  • Melancholy: 3 days spent feeling depressed (and that's being generous), leaving 3 days.
  • Light Reading & Movies: A few dozen minutes a day reading books or watching shows takes 2 days, leaving 1 day.
  • The Final Day: The last remaining day is your birthday—how on earth could anyone possibly study on their birthday?!

Now we finally understand a fundamental truth: How is it even possible for a student NOT to fail?!

Applied Logic

During a Logic class, the professor called on a student:

"Let's use an example. Does your family happen to own a cow?"

"Yes, sir..."

"Therefore, following strict logic, your family must own a pasture, right?"

"Right!"

"And following that same logic, you must have a house right next to the pasture?"

"Oh, absolutely!"

"Since you have a wonderful house by a pasture, you must be relatively well-off with some extra cash?"

"Naturally, Professor!"

"And because you have money, by logical deduction, you must have a girlfriend?"

"That's amazing, spot on..."

"And because you have a girlfriend, it follows logically that you are not homosexual! That is pure logic!"

Thrilled by how useful logic was, the student ran out to find his friend to show off:

"Hey, does your family own a cow?"

"No!"

"By strict logic... that means you must be gay!"

The School Subjects

Mathematics

The only subject with zero practical use. You learn that \(1+1=2\), only for someone to tell you a few years later that \(1+1=10\) and explain binary code. They also teach you calculus and derivatives, but at the end of the day, you still need a pocket calculator just to go grocery shopping.

Physics

The scientific study of falling apples and other fruits. You also learn how to calculate train schedules and figure out when two trains will collide if they run on the exact same track. People who finish studying physics rarely end up planting apple orchards or working as train conductors.

Chemistry

A subject entirely based on memorising correct answers and lab procedures. Pour liquid A into liquid B, shake or stir, sometimes heat it up, and finally dump the whole toxic mess out into the garden. That's a chemistry experiment.

Biology

The study of fruit flies and a few household pets. However, if you ask an adult where babies come from, you will inevitably be told that a stork delivered the baby and dropped it on the windowsill.

Geography

Teaches you how to read a map and point out America on a globe. This is perhaps the newest subject of all, because before Christopher Columbus discovered America, absolutely nobody had to study this.

History

Teachers force you to memorise who overthrew which king. Quite often, you have to memorise the birthdates of monarchs who never even threw birthday parties, and you certainly don't need to buy them gifts.

Literature

You are assigned to read a book so thick you barely have time to glance at the title before walking into the exam room. After finishing this subject, you learn that Victor Hugo and Viktor Krum are not brothers, and that Charles Dickens wasn't a stationery store owner—even though he made his entire living off a pen.

Philosophy

Philosophy is a phenomenological study of phenomena where sometimes when we study phenomena, it truly becomes phenomenological, which is why people call phenomenology the study of those phenomena—except when those phenomena aren't phenomenological, making the study of those phenomena actual phenomenology.

The Delayed Punchline

The lion of the jungle forced all the animals to submit one animal per day for it to eat, threatening to slaughter and sun-dry the entire forest if they refused. The animals held a meeting and passed a resolution: every day, they would hold a joke-telling contest. If the storyteller made all the animals laugh, they were spared. If even a single animal failed to laugh, the storyteller had to become the lion's dinner.

When it was the rabbit's turn, he told his joke. All the animals rolled on the ground laughing hysterically—except for the cow, who kept a completely straight face. So, off went the rabbit to be fed to the lion.

The next day, it was the chicken's turn. After the chicken finished telling its story, not a single animal laughed—except for the cow, who burst out laughing and fell over in stitches. The poor chicken was dragged away to the lion.

Puzzled, the animals crowded around the cow and asked, "The rabbit's joke yesterday was hilarious, why didn't you laugh? And the chicken's joke today was completely dry and unfunny, why were you laughing so hard?"

The cow blinked and replied, "What do you mean? I was just laughing at the rabbit's joke from yesterday. I didn't care about the chicken's joke at all."
